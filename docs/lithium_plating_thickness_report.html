Why a Thinner Anode Reduces Lithium Plating Risk

June 2, 2026

Addressing the M4.7 quiz question on anode thickness and electrolyte transport limitations

1. Your Intuition — and Where It Goes Wrong

The misconception: "A thinner electrode with the same diffusion coefficient would just produce a truncated version of the same concentration curve — so the concentration at the boundary should be the same."

This reasoning treats the concentration profile as if it were set by some fixed boundary condition that you then "draw inward" according to the diffusion coefficient. But the concentration profile across the electrode is not a simple exponential decay from a fixed boundary value. It arises from the coupled balance between ion transport and electrochemical reaction distributed across the entire electrode thickness.

2. The Physics: Transport–Reaction Coupling

During fast charging, $\text{Li}^+$ ions enter the anode from the separator and are consumed by intercalation reactions throughout the electrode volume. The steady-state electrolyte concentration profile is governed by:

$$D_{\text{eff}} \frac{\partial^2 c_e}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{1 - t_+^0}{F} \, j_{\text{rxn}}$$

where $j_{\text{rxn}}$ is the volumetric reaction current density (A/m³). For a galvanostatic charge at current $I$ with roughly uniform reaction distribution, $j_{\text{rxn}} = I / (A \cdot L)$, where $L$ is the electrode thickness.

Integrating this equation twice (with zero-flux BC at the current collector), the total concentration drop across the electrode is:

$$\Delta c_e \sim \frac{I \cdot L}{2 \, A \, D_{\text{eff}}}$$
Key result: The concentration drop across the electrode scales as $\Delta c_e \propto L$. A thinner electrode ⇒ smaller $\Delta c_e$ ⇒ more uniform electrolyte concentration across the electrode.

Why? Two effects work together when you thin the electrode:

  • Shorter diffusion path: $\text{Li}^+$ has less distance to travel to reach all active material.
  • Reaction redistribution: The volumetric reaction rate $j_{\text{rxn}} = I/(A \cdot L)$ increases, but over a shorter distance, the total flux demand at the boundary doesn’t change. The net effect is a smaller concentration variation.

3. Why This Matters for Lithium Plating

The chain of causation is:

  1. Large $\Delta c_e$ in electrolyte → $\text{Li}^+$ is depleted deep in the electrode
  2. $\text{Li}^+$ depletion → intercalation reaction concentrates near the separator surface (where $\text{Li}^+$ is still available)
  3. Concentrated reaction at surface → local solid-phase lithium saturation near the separator
  4. Local saturation → anode surface potential drops below 0 V vs. $\text{Li}/\text{Li}^+$ → metallic lithium plates out

A thinner electrode breaks this chain at step 1: smaller $\Delta c_e$ means more uniform electrolyte concentration, more uniform reaction distribution, and less risk of local saturation.

Analogy: Imagine a corridor where people enter from one end and must find seats distributed along its length. In a long corridor, people crowd near the entrance because it takes too long to walk to the far seats. In a short corridor, everyone spreads out evenly — same walking speed, but much less backup at the entrance. The corridor length determines the crowding, not just the walking speed.

4. Why "Same Curve, Just Shorter" Is Wrong

The student’s mental model implicitly assumes the concentration profile shape is an intrinsic property of the material (set by $D_{\text{eff}}$ alone). But the profile is a solution to a differential equation whose source term ($j_{\text{rxn}}$) depends on $L$. When you change $L$:

  • The volumetric reaction rate changes: $j_{\text{rxn}} = I/(A \cdot L)$
  • The domain length changes
  • These two effects don’t cancel — the concentration drop scales as $\Delta c_e \propto L$, not $L^0$

You cannot simply "truncate" the thick-electrode profile to get the thin-electrode profile. The entire profile is reshaped.

5. PyBaMM Simulation: DFN Model Confirmation

To confirm this, here is a Doyle–Fuller–Newman (DFN) simulation using PyBaMM with Chen2020 parameters. Three cases: baseline anode thickness (85 μm), 70% (60 μm), and 50% (43 μm).

Important methodological note: All three cells are charged at the same absolute current (7.6 A, the baseline cell’s 1.5C rate) rather than the same C-rate. This is critical: reducing electrode thickness while maintaining porosity reduces the total active material and hence cell capacity. Using the same C-rate would conflate two effects (shorter transport path and lower absolute current). By fixing the current, we isolate the transport path length as the only variable. The thinner electrodes are effectively being charged at higher C-rates (2.1C for 70%, 3.0C for 50%) — yet their concentration gradients are still smaller.

What the plots show:

  • Top-left (concentration vs. position): The thick anode (red, 85 μm) develops a large concentration gradient across the cell. Electrolyte is severely depleted in the anode interior while concentration builds on the cathode side. The thin anode (blue, 43 μm) maintains a far more uniform profile — despite being charged at an effective 3.0C.
  • Top-right (normalized position): Even when rescaled to the same $[0, 1]$ domain, the thick electrode shows larger absolute concentration swings, confirming the $\Delta c_e \propto L$ scaling.
  • Bottom-left (voltage): All cells are charged at the same 7.6 A. The thinner electrodes have less capacity and reach the 4.2 V cutoff sooner. The baseline electrode (red) takes the longest to charge because it has the most active material to fill.
  • Bottom-right (concentration at anode/separator interface): The thick anode shows the largest drop in local $\text{Li}^+$ concentration at the separator interface during charging. This depletion drives non-uniform intercalation and increases plating risk. The thin anode maintains higher local $\text{Li}^+$ availability throughout.
Bottom line: A thinner anode doesn’t just "truncate" the concentration profile — it fundamentally reduces the magnitude of the electrolyte concentration variation. Less concentration variation ⇒ more uniform intercalation ⇒ less local saturation ⇒ lower plating risk. The scaling is $\Delta c_e \propto L$: halve the thickness, halve the concentration drop.
