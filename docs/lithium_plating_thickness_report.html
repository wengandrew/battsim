Why a Thinner Anode Reduces Lithium Plating Risk

Addressing the M4.7 quiz question on anode thickness and electrolyte transport limitations

1. Your Intuition — and Where It Goes Wrong

The misconception: "A thinner electrode with the same diffusion coefficient would just produce a truncated version of the same concentration curve — so the concentration at the boundary should be the same."

This reasoning treats the concentration profile as if it were set by some fixed boundary condition that you then "draw inward" according to the diffusion coefficient. But the concentration profile across the electrode is not a simple exponential decay from a fixed boundary value. It arises from the coupled balance between ion transport and electrochemical reaction distributed across the entire electrode thickness.

2. The Physics: Transport–Reaction Coupling

During fast charging, Li+ ions enter the anode from the separator and are consumed by intercalation reactions throughout the electrode volume. The steady-state electrolyte concentration profile is governed by:

D_eff · d²c/dx² = −(1 − t+)/F · jrxn

where jrxn is the volumetric reaction current density (A/m³). For a galvanostatic charge at current I with roughly uniform reaction distribution, jrxn = I / (A · L), where L is the electrode thickness.

Integrating this equation twice (with zero-flux BC at the current collector), the total concentration drop across the electrode is:

Δc ∼ I · L / (2 · A · Deff)
Key result: The concentration drop across the electrode is proportional to L (electrode thickness). A thinner electrode ⇒ smaller Δc ⇒ more uniform electrolyte concentration across the electrode.

Why? Two effects work together when you thin the electrode:

  • Shorter diffusion path: Li+ has less distance to travel to reach all active material.
  • Reaction redistribution: The volumetric reaction rate jrxn = I/(A·L) increases, but over a shorter distance, the total flux demand at the boundary doesn't change. The net effect is a smaller concentration variation.

3. Why This Matters for Lithium Plating

The chain of causation is:

  1. Large Δc in electrolyte → Li+ is depleted deep in the electrode
  2. Li+ depletion → intercalation reaction concentrates near the separator surface (where Li+ is still available)
  3. Concentrated reaction at surface → local solid-phase lithium saturation near the separator
  4. Local saturation → anode surface potential drops below 0 V vs. Li/Li+ → metallic lithium plates out

A thinner electrode breaks this chain at step 1: smaller Δc means more uniform electrolyte concentration, more uniform reaction distribution, and less risk of local saturation.

Analogy: Imagine a corridor where people enter from one end and must find seats distributed along its length. In a long corridor, people crowd near the entrance because it takes too long to walk to the far seats. In a short corridor, everyone spreads out evenly — same walking speed, but much less backup at the entrance. The corridor length determines the crowding, not just the walking speed.

4. Why "Same Curve, Just Shorter" Is Wrong

The student's mental model implicitly assumes the concentration profile shape is an intrinsic property of the material (set by Deff alone). But the profile is a solution to a differential equation whose source term (jrxn) depends on L. When you change L:

  • The volumetric reaction rate changes: jrxn = I/(A·L)
  • The domain length changes
  • These two effects don't cancel — the concentration drop scales as L, not L0

You cannot simply "truncate" the thick-electrode profile to get the thin-electrode profile. The entire profile is reshaped.

What the plots show:

  • Top-left (concentration vs. position): The thick anode (red) develops a much larger concentration gradient. The electrolyte is severely depleted in the interior, while concentration piles up near the cathode side. The thin anode (blue) maintains a far more uniform profile.
  • Top-right (normalized position): Even when rescaled to the same [0, 1] domain, the thick electrode shows larger absolute concentration swings — confirming the Δc ∼ L scaling.
  • Bottom-left (voltage): The thick electrode charges at a lower voltage initially (more ohmic/transport loss) and hits the voltage cutoff sooner.
  • Bottom-right (concentration at anode/separator interface over time): The thick anode shows a dramatic drop in local Li+ concentration at the separator interface during charging. This depletion drives non-uniform reaction and increases plating risk. The thin anode maintains much higher local Li+ availability.
Bottom line: A thinner anode doesn't just "truncate" the concentration profile — it fundamentally reduces the magnitude of the electrolyte concentration variation. Less concentration variation ⇒ more uniform intercalation ⇒ less local saturation ⇒ lower plating risk. The scaling is Δc ∝ L: halve the thickness, halve the concentration drop.
